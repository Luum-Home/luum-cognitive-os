## What it is
An explanatory FAQ on Cognitive OS's license: FSL-1.1-MIT (Functional Source License 1.1 with an automatic MIT future-license conversion), covering the move from a prior Apache-2.0 posture, what is free vs. restricted, and how commercial use is treated.

## Key mechanics
- Short version: FSL-1.1-MIT permits reading, using, copying, modifying, forking, publishing, and redistributing Cognitive OS for free, including inside commercial products. The restriction: before the MIT conversion date you may not sell a product/service whose value derives entirely or substantially from Cognitive OS itself (i.e., no paid hosted/managed COS offering, paid support/hosting for COS as the product, or white-labelled resale). After the Change Date, the covered software automatically becomes MIT.
- Rationale for choosing FSL over Apache 2.0: keep source public and usable while preventing a better-capitalized third party from immediately launching a hosted COS service without contributing back; FSL-1.1-MIT chosen over more restrictive source-available licenses because it's short/readable, ties the restriction only to selling the software itself (not ordinary use), imposes no copyleft/network-disclosure obligations, converts automatically to MIT after two years, and fixes the future license now rather than leaving it to a later vendor decision.
- Conversion mechanics: automatic on the second anniversary of the date the covered software was first made available under FSL-1.1-MIT (the "Change Date"); for later-added material, the date that material was first published under FSL-1.1-MIT applies; conversion requires no new license-file publication and cannot be delayed for already-published code — only accelerated by the project choosing an earlier, more permissive license.
- Free-use list (11 items): internal company use, self-hosting, production use (own org or products where COS isn't the primary product), personal/educational/research/nonprofit use, inspect/audit/fork/modify, publish patches/forks under the same terms, redistribute with notices, use in your own dev workflow, use as a consulting tool (not sold as the product itself), embed where COS isn't the primary value, and use MIT-converted code freely after the Change Date. No license key, registration, usage reporting, royalty, or revenue share required.
- Comparison table vs. Apache 2.0: OSI-approval (No until conversion), internal use (same), modification/redistribution (same, with the Sell limitation), commercial products built with it (allowed if not substantially COS itself), selling hosted/managed COS (not allowed pre-conversion), copyleft (none either way), patent grant (Apache has an express grant; FSL text does not), future license (Apache already permissive; FSL auto-becomes MIT after two years).
- Explicit non-hidden disclosures: FSL-1.1-MIT is not OSI-approved as of the FAQ; pre-conversion status is source-available, not open source; it is less permissive than Apache 2.0 specifically for hosted/managed resale; prior Apache-2.0-distributed copies keep their original rights (no claw-back); the public source has no hidden proprietary replacement; the FAQ is not a side agreement — the LICENSE file controls.
- Commercial-use guidance: generally permitted examples (internal engineering use, self-hosted production, a SaaS product using COS behind the scenes for a distinct application, a vertical agent product embedding COS as infrastructure, consulting where COS is a tool, redistribution as part of a larger non-COS-resale product); generally requires a separate commercial arrangement (hosted COS, managed agent runtime, agent orchestration SaaS, control-plane SaaS for third-party agents, paid support/OEM/white-label resale, or any distribution whose primary value is COS itself rather than the buyer's own application). Commercial exceptions may be available from the project owner for these restricted cases; the public license itself creates no royalty/revenue-share program.
- Contributor terms: no copyright assignment; contributions are made under the repository license (FSL-1.1-MIT, same auto-MIT conversion), per `CONTRIBUTING.md`.
- Forward-looking commitment: the two-year MIT conversion for already-published code is built into the license and cannot be retroactively delayed; the project may move future code to a more permissive license earlier but does not intend to become more restrictive, and commits to publicly documenting any future license change with rationale, effective date, and downstream impact.
